**tl;dr** Locks. You’ll need at least **two really good locks**. You can
protect your frame and wheels, but be prepared to lose the “strippable” items
such as brakes, shifters, seats, and handlebars (though this can be mitigated
with special screws or magnetic balls). You’re probably better off with an
inexpensive bike rental service such as NYC’s [Citi
Bike](https://www.citibikenyc.com/) or San Francisco’s [Lyft
bikes](https://lyftbikes.com/pricing), which cost ~$17/month.

Over eighteen months ago I made the monumental decision to lock my bike
outside, overnight in the city. It can be done, but it’s not pretty. I’ll share
what I learned.

First, let me tell you about the three locks I use:

- [**Viro
  Euromonolith**](https://www.viro.it/en-gb/hardware/padlocks/euromonolith)
  padlock with [**Pewag’s ½" security
  chain**](https://www.westechrigging.com/pewag-security-chain-012-galv-kit-4.html).
  You’ve never seen a padlock like this—it’s the kind of padlock that makes a
  professional bike thief cry. It’s a 2-pound beast. Now, you may have trouble
  believing that anything manufactured in Italy would be considered the
  pinnacle of craftsmanship, but that would be a mistake: this lock is
  unbeatable. This lock-and-chain combo is heavy though (13.95 lbs.), so I
  don’t carry it around with me; it always stays on the bike rack at the corner
  of Washington and Polk streets.
- [**ABUS Ultimate
  420**](https://www.abus.com/usa/Products/Bicycle-locks/U-Locks-Bike/Ultimate-420)
  is my new favorite U-lock, which is lighter and more resistant to rust than
  my previous favorite, the [**Kryptonite New York Fahgettaboudit Mini
  U-Lock**](https://www.kryptonitelock.com/en/products/product-information/current-key/002178.html).
  It’s conveniently sized to fit in a back pocket.
- **ABUS BORDO Combo 6100** (discontinued). To be frank, this isn’t the lock
  that’s gonna protect your bike in San Francisco, but it will be enough to
  protect your basket/pannier, which is what I use it for.
- **ABUS BORDO GRANIT XPlus 6500** Folding Lock (discontinued) (honorable
  mention). This protected my bike for over six months until I moved to the
  two-lock system. It did a great job: the thieves tried to burn it (I saw the
  scorch marks), cut it with bolt cutters (I saw the grooves), break it with
  iron bars (I found the bars next to my bike, bent), and freeze it. This lock
  withstood it all without complaining. And then one morning I came out and the
  bike was gone, and so was the lock. And that’s one of the mysteries: the bike
  thieves always take the locks, and I’m not sure why.

Statistics (derived from a small sample set of one bike owner, me):

![Timeline of items stolen from my bike](/images/bike_lock_timeline.png)

_The above chart shows the timeline of stuff stolen from my bike (sometimes the
entire bike). The Viro padlock & chain (5/2020) and the proper placement of the
Kryptonite lock (8/2020) rendered the bike essentially theft-proof, but the
parts (seat, handlebars) were vulnerable until I placed magnetic balls wherever
a hex key would fit. The bike was off the street from 8/26/2020–12/21/2020._

- On any given night, my bike will probably _not_ be stolen. In fact, I
  averaged **104 days between incidents**. That is to say, for every day that
  something was stolen from my bike, there were 103 days when nothing was
  stolen. That means, given a reasonable set of locks, there’s less than a **1%
  chance** that something will get stolen (note that the real number is
  probably closer to 1.5% because some nights my bike wasn’t locked up outside,
  for example, when it’s in the shop waiting for the replacement handlebars to
  arrive).
- The average repair cost was **$351**, but this has trended downward since I
  learned the proper way to secure my frame and wheels: Since 9/8/2019, I’ve
  averaged **$152** per incident.
- I’ve averaged **$96/month** for the privilege of having an outdoor-parked
  private bike. Lyft is a much cheaper alternative at $17/month.

![My bike seat, the day before it was stolen](/images/bike_lock_seat.jpg)

_This picture of my bike seat was taken the day before they stole it. The seat,
post, and collar cost $101 to replace. I filled the Allen wrench cavities of
the replacement seat with crazy glue to prevent future theft. Later I switched
to using magnetic balls to fill the cavities. Note the yellow bike tape
“dogging” my bike seat: peeling it off is an orgasmic experience._

**What they steal**: other than the bikes, wheels, baskets, handlebars, brakes,
shifters, and seats, some of the items stolen defy comprehension. Yes, that’s
right: they steal crazy stuff. One time I came out to discover that the yellow
tape I had used to “dog” (make appear damaged/worthless) my bike seat had been
painstakingly removed. They must have carefully peeled the worthless yellow
electrical tape. I imagine a crystal meth user, a tweaker, up for several days,
unable to stop picking his face. Suddenly his eyes alight on the bike seat:
finally, something he can pick! He carefully picks at the bike tape, gently
tugging, peeling it back, the slow, clean separation of the tape from the bike
seat providing what can only be described as a near-orgasmic experience.

On two different occasions they stole the yellow plastic Caltrain label
(pictured above, “4th & King”). These items have no resale value—the Caltrain
conductors give them out for free—so it’s a mystery to me why the thieves take
them.

One time they stole the lock. Yes, that’s right: the bike thieves stole the
lock, not the bike. Go figure.

They don’t steal the bike seat as often as you might think; I’ve only had it
stolen once (I’m not counting the two other times when the entire bike was
stolen). And for several months I had a quick-release bike seat, which was even
easier to steal, and it was never taken.

They’ve never stolen the pedals.

![My bike with its rear wheel stolen](/images/bike_lock_rear_wheel.jpg)

_The morning of August 8, 2019, after thieves had stolen the rear wheel. This
was the single most expensive theft—the wheel alone cost $325 to replace (it
had an internally geared hub). I had locked the frame but neglected to secure
the rear wheel._

**Lock your bike properly:** I didn’t lock my bike properly: My rear wheel was
unprotected, the bike thieves stole it, and when all was said and done I was
out $483—$20 shy of the original purchase price of the bike!

The irony was that the wheel wasn’t valuable to the thieves. “It’s not a common
wheel,” said Gui of High Trails Cyclery. “They would have had a hard time
finding a buyer.” [0]

So how to lock your bike properly? Don’t ask me; ask the San Francisco Bike
Coalition: they have a great [web
page](https://sfbike.org/resources/bike-security-and-locking/#bike-locking)
describing several effective ways to lock your bike.

![Locking within the golden triangle](/images/bike_lock_golden_triangle.png)

_Make sure to lock within the golden triangle; it would have saved me almost
$500 had I done that._

The quick & dirty: make sure to lock your wheel within the golden triangle (see
picture).

The second lock, which is a chain, should be looped through the frame, through
the front wheel, and around the rack.

![A 4mm magnetic ball in the well of the head of the hex screw of the gear
shifter](/images/bike_lock_magnetic_ball.jpg)

_A 4mm magnetic ball in the well of the head of the hex screw prevents thieves
from using an Allen wrench to remove the shifter. The magnetic balls can be
removed with another magnet._

**Preventing my bike from getting stripped**: Now that I’ve secured the frame &
two wheels, I needed to secure the components that are easily stripped: seats,
handlebars, shifters, brakes. I’ve placed magnetic balls in the well heads of
the screws securing those components to prevent thieves from using an Allen
wrench to remove them. Unfortunately, there’s more than one size, so I used 1
✖️ 6mm (the handlebar screw, big), 4 ✖️ 5mm, several 4mm and several 2.5mm
balls. I purchased the balls from [Super Magnet
Man](https://supermagnetman.com/) with the exception of the 2.5mm variety,
which I purchased from [Speks](https://www.getspeks.com/). If you’re in my
neighborhood & are looking for 2.5mm or 4mm magnetic balls, look me up—I’ll be
happy to give you some of mine.

**Why lock my bike outside?** “You’d be crazy to lock your bike outside
overnight!” was the universal response when I told my friends I was considering
locking my bike outside.

It was the challenge: that’s why I did it. “I can make it work,” I thought,
“I’m smart, I’m an engineer, and this is an engineering problem.”

The other reason is that I didn’t want the bike in my apartment: my apartment
is small, and the bike is big.

![Map of reported bike thefts in San Francisco in
2016](/images/bike_lock_theft_map.png)

_The locations of the ~700 reported bike thefts in San Francisco in 2016,
courtesy of
[hoodline.com](https://hoodline.com/2017/07/hot-wheels-mapping-san-francisco-s-multi-million-dollar-bike-theft-industry).
My neighborhood isn’t the absolute worst for bike thefts, but it’s still pretty
bad._

I could have locked my bike in the apartment courtyard—my landlady had already
given me permission—but I found that the five minutes spent hauling the bike up
the courtyard stairs, down the apartment stairs, and through the vestibule was
enough to deter me from riding the bike—I rode Muni (SF mass transit) instead.
Plus, even when I kept my bike in the courtyard I had to continue locking
it—thieves might get into the courtyard when the sanitation workers didn’t
properly close the entrance.

**Addendum 2026-10-28**: Republished this post to my personal blog.

**Addendum 2021-5-5**: The bike thieves struck again: they stole the small ABUS
6100 combo lock. It was the weakest of the three locks, and served only to
protect the pannier. Also, it’s the most visible lock, and a casual thief might
be fooled into thinking it’s the only lock, discovering, once he has
successfully picked the lock, that there are two bigger locks remaining.

**Addendum 2020-12-21**: I’m locking my bike outside again. I’ve updated the
blog post to include the use of strategically placed magnetic balls to foil
bike thieves.

**Addendum 2020-08-26**: I’m not locking my bike outside; I’m locking it in the
courtyard. There’s a [global bike
shortage](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/05/18/nyregion/bike-shortage-coronavirus.html),
and I believe that has made my bike more attractive to thieves: they’re more
likely to strip my bike of brakes/seats/handlebars, and until I’ve developed a
plan to secure those, I’m gonna keep my bike off the street, overnight.
